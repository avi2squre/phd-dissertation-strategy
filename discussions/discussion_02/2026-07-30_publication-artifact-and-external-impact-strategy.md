## Why outputs mattered

**User-stated requirement, publicly generalized.** Internal strategic objectives favored methodological work capable of producing independently recognized research contributions, transferable technical skills, external adoption, and strong publication artifacts. A near-term fellowship report also required tangible evidence that the user had moved from coursework and preparation into active research.

The detailed private objective function is omitted. The causal consequence retained here is that the dissertation was evaluated not only as a thesis but also as a program capable of producing papers, reusable software, credible demonstrations, and evidence of independent use.

## Publication count and venue corrections

**Later correction or qualification.** Stream 02 rejected an earlier plan centered on 6-10 papers, several flagship venue acceptances, multiple clinical papers, a shared task, and press coverage. It was described as a possible exceptional outcome but a poor planning baseline because it encouraged thin slicing and treated acceptance, publicity, and external recognition as controllable.

The July 29 discussion offered two partially overlapping alternatives:

- a dissertation core of three strong, coherent first-author papers, possibly with one additional position or resource paper and meaningful collaborations;
- a broader aspiration of four to six lead-author outputs, including one or two flagship methods papers, an adopted benchmark or dataset, a reusable evaluation/control system, and a clinical or biological validation paper.

**Qualification.** The archive did not reconcile these counts, and neither was accepted as a fixed target. The stable principle was coherence and external value rather than volume.

Major ML or NLP venues were treated as useful targets when the methodological contribution fit. They were not treated as evidence by themselves that a contribution was important, nor as outcomes a dissertation plan could guarantee.

## Research-scientist versus research-engineering evidence

**Stream 02 factual claim based on historical job-description checks.** First-author publications and sustained research ownership were described as strong signals for research-scientist paths. Production Python, distributed or scalable evaluation, statistical experimental design, debugging, model/data versioning, testing, containers, and deployment-quality systems were described as additional requirements for research-engineering paths.

**Stream 02 recommendation.** The desired output combination was therefore:

> first-author methodological research + a technically serious open-source system that other researchers actually use.

A paper-only portfolio with notebook-level code was judged incomplete for engineering roles. An infrastructure-only portfolio without an independent methods contribution was judged weaker for research-scientist and academic roles.

## Software as a research artifact

The archive used an existing public visualization project as a concrete example. It judged the project promising but not yet mature evidence of external impact because it lacked releases, automated tests, continuous integration, a synthetic test dataset, reproducible dependency locking, formal citation metadata, and independent adoption.

**Stream 02 recommendation.** A stronger research-software release would include:

- synthetic or clearly public test data;
- configuration rather than hard-coded local paths;
- unit and integration tests;
- continuous integration;
- an executable demonstration;
- a reproducible environment;
- release notes, changelog, and citation metadata;
- an archived version and permanent identifier where appropriate.

**Later correction or qualification.** A permanent identifier improves preservation and citability but does not make software peer reviewed, establish adoption, or prove major impact.

## External use and recognition

Stream 02 distinguished documentation from independent impact. A repository, polished README, release, or self-created archive is evidence that an artifact exists. Stronger evidence would come from:

- independent users, contributors, or replications;
- citations or documented reuse;
- adoption by another research group;
- invitations to present, review, or advise;
- external feedback tied to a concrete use;
- competitive selection or recognized service;
- changes to another group's research or deployment practice.

**Stream 02 strategic judgment.** One widely used contribution could be more valuable than several lightly used publications. External adoption was therefore both a portfolio objective and a test of whether the artifact solved a real problem.

## Near-term fellowship evidence

**Stream 02 factual claim based on the historical fellowship documents.** The fellowship rubric did not require an accepted paper by the next review. Multiple outputs, including software, or a substantial data/analysis milestone could support a strong evaluation.

The recommended package was a coherent set of evidence rather than a rushed manuscript:

1. a reproducible software release;
2. a bounded funded-project package such as a protocol, literature matrix, baseline plan, data-readiness analysis, or pilot result;
3. a short dissertation research-agenda memo;
4. a presentation, demonstration, poster draft, or manuscript outline;
5. an evidence index recording dates, roles, status, links, verification, privacy class, impact, and next milestone.

Administrative onboarding was explicitly distinguished from research output. A paper should not be described as in progress until scope and prospective authorship had been discussed with collaborators.

The service-leadership website discussion is not reproduced in detail because it did not materially alter the dissertation direction. Its relevant lesson was to timebox service work and document real organizational use rather than treating code alone as impact.
